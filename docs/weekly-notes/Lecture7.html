Lecture 7

Regression Diagnostics and Log Transformations

Regression Diagnostics

  1. First, we look at the \(F\) statistic and the associated \(p\)-value. The \(F\) statistic is an omnibus test, which tests \(H_0\) that none of the independent variables in the model is a significant predictor of the dependent variable.
  2. Next, look at the \(p\)-value associated with each independent variable.
  3. If the predictor is significant, we look at the sign and value of the \(\hat{\beta}\) for that predictor. The sign of \(\hat{\beta}\) tells us if it’s a direct or inverse relationship between \(x\) and \(y\).

Two or more categorical variables in a regression

Imagine we want to predict median house value using these predictors:

  • % of individuals with a bachelor’s degree or more

  • Presence of a park (binary)

  • Police unit covering the block group (categorical in A, B, C, and D)

  • Parking type (categorical, type of parking)

There will always be \(n-1\) predictors for \(n\) categories in a predictor.

  • If Unit A = 0, Unit B = 0, Unit C = 0, then it must be the case that the Police Unit must be Unit D.

  • Since D is the reference category, the \(\hat{\beta}\) values are all in comparison to D.

\(\beta_0\) represents median household value where A, B, and C and are all equal to zero (so it’s the median household income where the block group is D). The equation would be:

\[ y=\text{Agg. Assaults}=\beta_0+\beta_1\text{Med. HVal.}+\beta_2\text{Parks}+\beta_3\text{DummyA} + \beta_4\text{DummyB}+\beta_5\text{DummyC} \]

When we receive the outputs from R, we plug in the coefficients to make a prediction.

\[ \hat{y}=30.21-0.56\text{Med. HVal.}+9.31\text{Parks}+0.13\text{DummyA}-1.27\text{DummyB}+0.34\text{DummyC} \]

\[ \hat{y}=30.21 - 0.56(35) + 9.31(0) + 0.13(0) -1.27(0)+0.34(0) = 10.61 \]

In this case, the model over-predicts the value of Aggravated Assaults by \(0.61\).

Regression Assumptions

Assumption Assumption Check
Linear relationship between dependent variable \(y\) and each predictor \(x\) Scatterplots between \(y\) and \(x\)

Normality of residuals

\(\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)\)

 Look at the histogram of the residuals to see if they are normal
Homoscedasticity. The variance of the residuals \(\epsilon\) is constant regardless of the values of each \(x\) Look at the scatterplots of standardized residuals against each predictor and see if variance of residuals remains the same for different values of each predictor
Independence of observations. There should be no spatial or temporal dependence in the data. Look at the Moran’s \(I\) of the residuals or the values of \(y\) to examine whether the regression residuals, or the dependent variable itself, are spatially autocorrelated.
No multicolinearity (applies only to multiple regression). Look at the correlation matrix of predictors. If correlation between any pair of predictors \(>0.8\) we might have multicolinarity. Alternatively regress each predictor against eachother and look at the \(R^2\) (should not be \(>0.8\)) or the \(\text{VIF}\).
No fewer than 10 observations per predictor. Look at the ratio between the # observations and # of predictors.

Calculating the Variance Inflation Factor

\[ \text{VIF}(x_j)=\frac{1}{1-R^2_j} \]

  1. For each predictor \(X_j\), regress \(X_j\) on the other predictors.
  2. \(R^2_j\) is the \(R^2\) from that auxiliary regression.
  3. Then compute the \(\text{VIF}\) from the formula above.

Example in R

Checking Linearity

options(scipen = 999)
multregdata <- read.csv('RegressionData.csv')
plot(multregdata$MEDHHINC, multregdata$MEDHVAL)
plot(multregdata$MEDHHINC, multregdata$PCTVACANT)
plot(multregdata$MEDHHINC, multregdata$PCTBACHMOR)

What we’re trying to avoid is very obvious patterns where the relationship is not linear (ex. it’s a parabola).

A review of logarithms

Logarithms are the opposite of exponentiation.

\[ \text{if } x=b^y,\text{ then }y=\text{log}_b(x) \]

When the base is \(e\), we have a natural logarithm denoted by \(\text{ln}\).

\(\text{log}\) transformations

Sometimes, it is possible to make a variable normal by subjecting it to some simple algebraic operations.

  • It can help with a non-normality of residuals, non-linear relationship between \(x\) and \(y\), or heteroskedasdicity.
  • For \(x>1\), \(\text{ln}(x)\) increases as \(x\) increases, but at a slower pace.
multregdata$LNMEDHHINC <- log(multregdata$MEDHHINC + 1)
